\section{Background}

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide, and \gls{nsclc} accounts for the majority of diagnosed cases \parencite{Sung2021,Herbst2018}. \gls{lusc} has historically constituted a major proportion of \gls{nsclc} diagnoses in Nepal whereas \gls{luad} is the more prevalent subtype globally. Unlike \gls{luad}, where drug targets against EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 have transformed clinical management, \gls{lusc} is still commonly treated using surgery, radiotherapy, platinum-based chemotherapy, and immunotherapy depending on disease stage and patient condition \parencite{Herbst2018,Lau2022-hy} as approved targeted therapies for \gls{lusc} patients remain fewer.

\gls{lusc} is not a uniform disease because large-scale molecular studies have shown extensive genomic, transcriptomic, epigenetic, and proteomic heterogeneity within. Early transcriptomic work has identified four reproducible \gls{lusc} mRNA expression subtypes: classical, basal, secretory, and primitive \parencite{Wilkerson2010}. The landmark publication by \gls{tcga} Research Network revealed alterations in pathways involving TP53, SOX2, PIK3CA, CDKN2A, NFE2L2, KEAP1, and squamous differentiation biology \parencite{Hammerman2012}. These subtypes differ in biological programs such as xenobiotic metabolism, squamous lineage differentiation, immune response, and proliferation.

While subtype definitions in \gls{lusc} were originally derived from gene expression, modern cancer cohorts such as \gls{tcga} contain multiple matched molecular data layers, including gene expression, \gls{dna} methylation, \gls{mirna} expression, \gls{cnv}, and protein abundance; which could be leveraged to understand the biological basis of subtype identity and relationship between these layers. Each omics modality measures a different level of tumour biology. Gene expression reflects transcriptional state, methylation captures epigenetic regulation, \gls{mirna} profiles reflect post-transcriptional regulation, \gls{cnv} reflects structural genomic alteration, and \gls{rppa} captures selected protein-level signals. Similarly, somatic mutation offers an independent and orthogonal view of tumor biology, capturing genetic alterations that may underlie the transcriptional and proteomic differences observed across LUSC molecular subtypes.

Machine learning has transformed computational biology and precision oncology across diverse scales of biological organisation. At the molecular sequence level, AlphaFold2 has achieved near-experimental accuracy in protein structure prediction from amino acid sequence alone \parencite{Jumper2021}, demonstrating that deep learning models trained on biological data can capture complex structural dependencies invisible to classical methods. Transformer-based genomic sequence models, including DNABERT \parencite{Ji2021}, learn contextual representations of \gls{dna} sequences transferable to regulatory element prediction and variant effect estimation. At the transcriptomic level, cell-state foundation models pre-trained on tens of millions of single-cell expression profiles - including GeneFormer \parencite{Theodoris2023} and scGPT \parencite{Cui2024}- enable few-shot cell-type annotation and in silico perturbation prediction with minimal labelled data. In cancer genomics, supervised and ensemble machine learning methods have been applied to molecular subtype classification, survival prediction, and multi-omics biomarker discovery across large
cohorts \parencite{Hoadley2018,Picard2021,Nicora2020}. These approaches have achieved impressive predictive performance; however, biological interpretability remains a central and unresolved challenge in clinical omics research \parencite{Janssen2024}. Furthermore, foundation model and deep learning approaches typically require thousands to tens of thousands of labelled training examples - a constraint that restricts their applicability to the small-sample, high-dimensional setting of individual \gls{tcga} modality cohorts, where available labelled samples range from 76 to 178 in the present study.

Models must also be interpreted to understand which genes, probes, \gls{mirna}s, or proteins are driving classification and whether those features correspond to known or plausible \gls{lusc} biology. Machine learning offers a useful framework for this comparison because supervised classifiers can quantify how well each omics modality predicts known molecular subtype labels. Interpretable approaches such as \gls{shap} provide one route for connecting model predictions to biological features \parencite{Lundberg2017,Lundberg2020}.

This thesis therefore focuses on comparative, interpretable machine learning across multiple omics platforms in TCGA-\gls{lusc}. Rather than proposing a new subtype system, it asks how well established \gls{lusc} molecular subtypes can be recovered from different molecular layers, whether different model architectures prioritise similar or different features, and whether modality-specific features converge. Additionally, it also examines whether subtype-discriminative features identified through SHAP analysis are reflected in subtype-associated patterns of somatic mutation.

\section{Problem Statement}

Although classical, basal, secretory, and primitive \gls{lusc} subtypes are well established at the transcriptomic level, it remains unclear how faithfully these subtype identities are encoded in other omics modalities. Studies often analyse one molecular layer or integrate multiple layers directly, but fewer studies compare modalities side by side using a common supervised learning framework. As a result, it is difficult to determine whether gene expression, methylation, \gls{mirna}, CNV, and RPPA contribute equivalent or distinct information for subtype classification.

Another unresolved issue is the influence of machine learning architecture on biological interpretation. Different algorithms may achieve similar classification performance while prioritising different molecular features. If biomarker conclusions are drawn from only one model type, the resulting feature list may reflect model-specific bias rather than stable subtype biology. Furthermore, feature-importance methods are often reported without assessing their stability across training folds, which is a concern in high-dimensional omics datasets with limited sample sizes.

Therefore, there is a need for a systematic analysis that compares multiple omics modalities, evaluates multiple machine learning architectures, interprets model features, and examines whether the resulting biological signals are stable and convergent across models and molecular layers.

\section{Rationale}

The rationale for this study is threefold. First, identifying which omics platforms best preserve \gls{lusc} subtype identity can guide future biomarker discovery and study design. If one modality consistently outperforms others, it may be prioritised for subtype classification. Conversely, modalities with weaker predictive performance may still provide complementary biological insight.

Second, comparing multiple machine learning models within each modality allows assessment of whether different model architectures converge on shared biological signals. This is important because model interpretability is increasingly used to nominate candidate biomarkers, yet such nominations are more reliable when supported across models or pathways rather than by one algorithm alone.

Third, pathway enrichment of interpreted features can help move beyond individual marker lists. Direct overlap of genes across omics layers may be limited because different platforms measure different molecular entities. Pathway-level analysis can reveal whether distinct features still converge on common biological processes relevant to \gls{lusc} subtype identity.
